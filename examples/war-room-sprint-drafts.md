## Slot 2 · Paid Media Truths — POV 18
**LinkedIn post**
> Most paid media problems aren't media problems.
>
> We obsess over the media part — bids, audiences, account structure, creative testing. That's the job and we take it seriously.
>
> But paid media amplifies the strengths *and* the weaknesses of the entire customer journey. And the weaknesses usually live outside the ad account.
>
> Weak creative. A slow website. Unclear positioning. A conversion flow that leaks. A CRM that quietly drops leads. Expectations no budget could meet.
>
> No bid strategy saves a landing page that takes six seconds to load. "More leads" makes a broken CRM worse, not better.
>
> So when performance stalls, we don't start with the campaign. We start with the journey — and more often than not, the biggest win isn't in the media at all.
>
> The best media optimization is often fixing the thing that isn't the media.
>

## Slot 3 · Measurement That Matters — POV 14 *(flagship — needs cleared numbers)*
**LinkedIn article + carousel**

### Article
> Pushing for a higher ROAS every year was quietly working against the client's actual goal.
>
> Here's the trap: ROAS is a great efficiency metric, and stakeholders love it. It's easy to understand and easy to justify budget with. So every year, the ask was the same — make it higher.
>
> The problem is that more efficiency and more growth pull in opposite directions. Squeeze ROAS up and you shrink the very spend that drives new revenue.
>
> So we asked the client directly: why push for more efficiency when we've shown, repeatedly, that it works against your growth? The honest answer — ROAS is the number their ownership and funders understand.
>
> That reframed everything. Instead of fighting over the target, we changed the number we were managing to.
>
> We modelled [lodging revenue] at different budget tiers — a figure every stakeholder already trusts — then did the same for LTV and new-customer acquisition, and trended them out. Now the conversation sounds like: *at this budget tier, expect [X] new customers, LTV grows [X]%, and over two years that compounds into [X]% more revenue.*
>
> ROAS didn't disappear. It moved. It stopped being the goal and became the floor — the minimum for profitability — while we set benchmarks on the growth segments that actually move the business.
>
> ROAS isn't the goal. It's the floor.
>
> — War Room

## Slot 4 · Programmatic — POV 11
**LinkedIn article**
> Programmatic isn't buying impressions. It's buying probability.
>
> Every DSP pitch sounds identical: "reach the right person, with the right message, at the right time." It's become wallpaper.
>
> And the infrastructure behind it is real — millions of media decisions made in real time, at a scale no human could manage by hand.
>
> But programmatic doesn't *create* relevance. It gives you the machinery to make those millions of decisions — and each one is only as smart as the data, signals, creative, measurement, and business rules you feed it.
>
> So the goal was never volume. Every impression should do one thing: increase the probability of a business outcome. Scale that isn't moving the odds is just a bigger invoice.
>
> You're not buying impressions. You're buying probability — so stop optimizing for how many, and start optimizing the odds.
>
> — War Room

## Slot 7 · Measurement That Matters — POV 15
**LinkedIn post**
> Last-click gives the credit. It doesn't tell you what was incremental.
>
> Every attribution model is flawed — but last-click is the most seductive one. It hands 100% of the credit to the final touch and tells you nothing about what would have happened anyway.
>
> That branded search that "converted"? The customer was already coming. Last-click just took the photo at the finish line and called itself the coach.
>
> The uncomfortable part: most brands aren't running incrementality testing. So they keep scaling channels that *look* efficient but may just be claiming credit for demand that already existed.
>
> If you've never held out a geo or a segment to see what happens without the spend, you don't know what's incremental. You only know what was last.
>
> Stop asking what got the last click. Start asking what wouldn't have happened without you.
>
> — War Room
